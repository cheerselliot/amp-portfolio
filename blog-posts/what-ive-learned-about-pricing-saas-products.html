What I've learned about pricing SaaS products

Monday 7 September 2026

Here is the simplest definition I have found that actually holds up: the price of something is the amount someone is willing to pay for it.

That sounds obvious. It is also why pricing is such a flaky topic. There is no clear answer. No formula that spits out the right number. At the end of the day, you are guessing. You can make an educated guess — and you should — but it is still a guess.

That is what makes pricing different from a lot of other decisions in business. In many areas you can gather most of the information before you commit. Pricing is not like that. You can research, benchmark, model, and debate until you are blue in the face. Then you still have to put a number in front of a customer and find out what happens.

Pricing has to be tested and tried in the marketplace. Everything else is preparation.

I have spent a lot of time recently thinking about how to commercialise a SaaS offering properly. Not just "what should we charge," but the whole chain underneath it: what are we selling, what does the subscription actually cover, what stays billable as Professional Services, and how do we avoid painting ourselves into a corner with early customers.

This post is two parts. First, a high-level commercialisation process I keep coming back to. Second, the personal learnings that stuck — especially the one idea that sits underneath all of it: relative.

A high-level commercialisation process

Before you debate price, you need a process that makes the debate useful. This is the sequence I use. None of it invents certainty. It just helps you make a better guess before the market tells you the truth.

Validate the opportunity. Confirm customer demand, the target customer profile, and the business problem being solved. If you cannot explain who wants this and why, pricing is the wrong conversation to be having.

Research the market. Benchmark competitors, pricing, positioning, and customer expectations. You are not copying anyone's number. You are building a frame of reference so your own number does not float in a vacuum.

Define the offering. Determine the MVP feature set and separate core capabilities from future enhancements. Packaging decisions get a lot easier when you know what is in the box today versus what is on the roadmap.

Estimate delivery effort. Assess implementation, support, and ongoing maintenance. Critically, distinguish reusable product work from customer-specific effort. Those two categories should almost never be priced the same way.

Define the support model. Decide what the recurring SaaS fee covers — support, maintenance, API upgrades, forced vendor changes — versus what remains billable Professional Services work. Get this wrong and you will either undercharge forever or surprise customers later. Neither is fun.

Design the commercial model. Decide packaging (bundles versus add-ons), recurring SaaS versus one-time services, pricing structure, and how implementation complexity influences commercial terms.

Validate internally. Review assumptions with Product, Engineering, Professional Services, and Sales. Use historical deals as reference points rather than precedents. Past deals are useful signals. They are not sacred cows.

Launch and iterate. Start with a simple offering, learn from early customers, standardise implementations, and evolve pricing and packaging over time. This is where the guess becomes evidence.

One practical tip I would add at the start of all of this: book the follow-ups at the outset. Commercialisation work dies in silence. If you leave alignment meetings to "we'll schedule something later," you will still be debating assumptions three months in.

Personal learnings

The process above helps you structure the work. These are the principles that changed how I think about the price itself.

Pricing is a relative concept. There is no absolute truth to it.

This is the big one. A number on its own means almost nothing. There is no universal "right" price sitting out there waiting to be discovered. Price only makes sense relative to something else.

Relative to what the customer is going to get.

Relative to the other things they still need to pay for in order to make the whole thing work — integrations, implementation, third-party products, change management, ongoing support.

Relative to the overall cost of the customer getting what they want. Your fee is rarely the whole bill. If your module is a small slice of a much larger investment, that changes how customers hear the number. If your fee is the main ticket, that changes it again.

Relative to what competitors charge in the marketplace.

Relative to what the customer already pays for your whole offering — other modules, seats, services, renewals. Your new price lives inside an existing commercial relationship, not outside it.

And relative to value: what the customer actually gets compared with what they are paying. Revenue gains. Cost savings. Risk removed. Time freed up. Without that comparison, "expensive" and "cheap" are just feelings.

Once you treat pricing as relative, the conversation gets more honest. You stop asking "does this feel like the right number?" and start asking "right relative to what?"

That is also why willingness to pay matters so much. Cost-plus thinking makes you feel in control, but customers do not buy your effort. They buy an outcome at a price they can justify against the alternatives.

Clearly define what the SaaS fee includes.

A key consideration is maintenance. If a third-party vendor deprecates an API or forces an upgrade, does the subscription cover that work, or is it charged separately as Professional Services?

This sounds like a legal detail. It is not. It is a product decision with commercial consequences. Ambiguity here is how you accidentally build a services business inside your SaaS fee — or how you create conflict with customers the first time something breaks outside your control.

It is much easier to reduce a price than increase one.

Start with a stronger anchor and negotiate down if required. Going the other direction — trying to lift a price after customers have already calibrated to a lower number — is painful for everyone involved.

Avoid introducing multiple tiers too early.

A single, well-defined offering is easier to understand, sell, and validate. Tiers feel sophisticated, but early on they often just multiply confusion. You end up debating packaging before you have proved willingness to pay for the core thing.

Use early customer implementations as learning loops.

Early customers are not just revenue. They are the best laboratory you have for validating willingness to pay, refining the offering, and converting bespoke work into reusable product capabilities over time. Remember: until the market responds, you are still guessing. Early deals are how you turn that guess into something sturdier.

That last point connects tightly to something I wrote about in From Service-Led to Product-Led. When you keep absorbing customer-specific work into the product without a plan, you stay stuck in a services mindset. The goal is to learn from the bespoke work, then deliberately turn the repeatable parts into product. Pricing and packaging should reinforce that shift — not fight it.

If I had to leave you with one idea, it would be this: pricing is relative. There is no absolute answer. Make the best educated guess you can, then test it — and keep the relative value story clear enough that customers can decide whether the trade is worth it.
